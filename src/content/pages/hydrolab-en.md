The hydro laboratory was built in Star City specifically to prepare cosmonauts for extravehicular activity: the first training sessions took place on 29 December 1978, and the official opening followed on 1 March 1980. Inside a cylindrical pool 23 meters across and 12 meters deep, full-scale mockups of orbital station modules sit on a movable platform — Salyut and Mir in earlier years, the International Space Station today.

A cosmonaut in an Orlan DMA-GN suit balanced to neutral buoyancy (the training suit weighs about 120 kilograms with equipment) spends hours underwater rehearsing the operations of an upcoming spacewalk — a typical session lasts about three hours. It is supported by a team of more than sixty specialists: doctors, instructors and divers who accompany the cosmonaut and regulate the suit's buoyancy and loads.

This was Alexander Shurubkin's work for eighteen years — from 1979 to 1997 — as he rose from senior test engineer to head of the research and testing laboratory. Over those years, more than sixty cosmonauts and astronauts, including European program participants and NASA crews, went through weightlessness training under his guidance and with his participation.

Working in simulated weightlessness demands the highest standards of safety, methodology and precision in every movement. This very experience became the foundation of the professional diver and instructor training methodology Alexander develops today as a PADI Course Director: a training system fit for a space program can certainly produce the finest diving instructors.

After a major renovation that began in 2014, the hydro laboratory resumed cosmonaut training in May 2020 — the first session featured a mockup of the ISS's newest module.
